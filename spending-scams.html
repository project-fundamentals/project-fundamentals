Scams & 'too good to be true'

Youth-targeted schemes, and the red flags that give them away.

By the end of this page

  • The scam types actually aimed at teenagers
  • How urgency and fake social proof get you to act before checking
  • Exactly what to do when something looks wrong

The line between aggressive marketing and an outright scam is thinner than it looks. Scams use the exact same emotional triggers as ordinary ads, turned up further and pointed at your savings.

What teenagers actually get targeted with

  • Youth-targeted scams — fake job offers, "become a reseller", gaming account trades, and giveaway impersonation.
  • High-risk money schemes — anything promising guaranteed or unusually high returns, often with a recruitment element.
  • Investing, speculation and gambling presented as the same thing — the most common confusion, and the most expensive one.

How scams manipulate

Two triggers do most of the work. Urgency compresses your decision time so that checking feels like it costs you the opportunity. Social proof supplies fake reassurance — screenshots of profits, comment sections full of thanks, a friend's hacked account vouching for it.

Both are aimed at the same target: getting you to act before you verify.

Why risk gets misjudged

Teenagers tend to misjudge risk by focusing on the reward and ignoring the probability of loss. A scheme advertising a 10x return gets evaluated on the 10x, not on the realistic chance of losing everything.

Understanding risk properly helps in two ways: identifying which opportunities are realistic rather than dangerous, and avoiding losses that are hard to recover from at this age.

Slowing down and asking "why is this so urgent?" is often enough on its own. A legitimate opportunity survives a day of checking. A scam usually does not.

What to do if something looks wrong

  • Do not reply, click through, or send anything — including "just to check".
  • Verify independently through official channels, never through a link the offer gave you.
  • Tell a parent or teacher early. Scams rely on embarrassment to keep victims quiet.
  • In Singapore, suspected scams can be reported to the police, and the ScamShield resources are a useful first check.
