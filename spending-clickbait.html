Spending

Clickbait & marketing tactics

How ads and trends are engineered to bypass your judgment.

What you'll learn

  • The four persuasion tactics behind most ads and clickbait
  • Why clickbait works on everyone — including you
  • One question that defuses most manipulative content

Marketing strategies and online trends are designed to create urgency and desire before you have had time to think a purchase through. That is not an accident or a side effect — it is the entire function of the design.

The tactics, named

Once you can name a tactic, it loses most of its power. These four cover the majority of what you will meet online.

  • Manufactured urgency — countdown timers, "only 3 left", "offer ends tonight". The scarcity is usually artificial and resets daily.
  • Social proof — "10,000 sold", visible review counts, influencer endorsement. Popularity is presented as evidence of quality.
  • Anchoring — a crossed-out "original" price next to the sale price. The high number is there to make the lower one feel like a gain rather than a cost.
  • Engineered curiosity — headlines and thumbnails built to leave a gap your brain wants closed. Clickbait is the pure form of this.

Why clickbait works on everyone

Clickbait is not a trick played on gullible people. It exploits a normal feature of attention: an unresolved question is uncomfortable, and clicking resolves it. The discomfort is real even when you know exactly what is being done to you.

What changes with awareness is not the pull — it is what you do in the two seconds afterwards.

Ordinary advertising and outright scams draw on the same emotional triggers. The difference is one of degree, not of kind — which is why learning to spot marketing tactics also makes you harder to scam.

A quick test

Before acting on something you have just seen online, ask: what does the person showing me this gain if I click, buy, or share? The answer is usually available, and it usually explains the framing.
