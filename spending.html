Spending Psychology & Clickbait

You spend more than you mean to. Here's why.

Teenagers overspend even when they know basic financial advice — because most overspending isn't a knowledge problem, it's a psychology problem.

Four things working against you

Impulse buying Instant gratification beats long-term thinking.

Teenagers are wired to prioritise instant gratification and underestimate long-term costs. That's not a character flaw — it's how the teenage brain weighs immediate reward against a delayed one. Recognising the pull in the moment is the first defence against it.

Social pressure Peers and online trends shape what feels "necessary."

Purchases don't happen in a vacuum. What friends have, what's trending online, and fear of missing out all quietly raise the bar for what feels like a normal thing to buy — even when nothing about the purchase itself changed.

Digital payments hide the cost Tapping a card doesn't feel like spending money.

Paying with cash makes the loss of money physical and immediate. Tapping a card or phone removes that friction — digital payments reduce the "pain of paying," which makes it easier to spend without registering that real money left your account.

Marketing and clickbait Ads and online trends are built to bypass your judgment.

Marketing strategies and online trends are designed to create urgency and desire before you've had time to think it through. The same emotional triggers — urgency, social proof, fear of missing out — show up in ordinary ads and in outright scams.

When persuasion becomes a scam

The line between aggressive marketing and an outright scam is thinner than it looks. Scams use the exact same emotional triggers as ordinary ads — just turned up further.

Common risks

  • Youth-targeted scams
  • High-risk "get rich quick" money schemes
  • Confusing legitimate investing with speculation or gambling

How scams manipulate

  • Urgency ("offer ends in 10 minutes")
  • Social proof ("thousands already joined")
  • A focus on the reward while glossing over the odds or the catch

Teenagers tend to misjudge risk by focusing on the reward and ignoring the probability of loss. Slowing down and asking "why is this so urgent?" is often enough to spot the pattern.

Before you buy, ask yourself

  • Would I still want this if I saw it again tomorrow?
  • Am I buying this because I need it, or because of how it's being marketed to me?
  • If a friend described this deal to me, would it sound realistic?
  • Is there urgency being placed on me to decide right now?
